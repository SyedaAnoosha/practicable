### Q001 — We Have a Risk Register, But No One Uses It

| Tag | Value |
|---|---|
| Domain | Risk |
| Effort | Moderate |
| Duration | 6-12 weeks |
| Tier | Foundational |
| Regulator pressure | Low |
| Cost | Low ($) |
| ROI horizon | Quick |
| Leadership traits | Accountability · Change · Collaboration |

**Plain-language question:** How do you make a risk register that people actually use?

**Answer:**

Most risk registers fail because they live in a spreadsheet that is owned by the risk team and read by no-one. The fix is to make the register *useful in decisions people are already making*, not a parallel artefact for compliance.

Five moves change the dynamic. First, link every risk to a live business objective so it ties to something the executive cares about. Second, assign business owners, not risk team members - risk facilitates, the business owns. Third, surface the top risks in monthly operating meetings with trend arrows, not in a quarterly risk-only forum. Fourth, embed the register where decisions happen - strategy reviews, project gates, investment committees. Fifth, archive stale risks ruthlessly; a register of 400 risks signals nothing, a register of 25 live risks demands attention.

ISO 31000 frames this as integrating risk into governance and decision-making rather than treating it as a process. Practitioners who get this right keep the register short, current, and visibly used by the people whose names are on it.

### Q002 — What's Our Risk Appetite Again?

| Tag | Value |
|---|---|
| Domain | Risk |
| Effort | Moderate |
| Duration | 6-12 weeks |
| Tier | Foundational |
| Regulator pressure | Moderate |
| Cost | Low ($) |
| ROI horizon | Quick |
| Leadership traits | Change · Strategic |

**Plain-language question:** How do you clarify risk appetite and tolerance so they actually work?

**Answer:**

Risk appetite often reads as a one-page document everyone agrees with and no-one applies. The cause is that appetite is written abstractly ("moderate", "low") without thresholds anyone can act on. The fix is to make appetite *operational*.

Define appetite per category - financial, safety, cyber, conduct, strategic - with one sentence of intent and one numeric trigger each. Give red/amber/green examples of what that means in practice. Stress-test the statement with three real recent decisions: would you have approved them under this appetite? If yes to all, the appetite is too loose. Link breaches to a defined escalation flow so a red zone is not just a colour but a trigger.

Review post-incident and annually. Train managers with role-play cases, not slide decks. Align KRIs and KPIs to appetite zones so the data flowing through dashboards is the same data the appetite is set against. ISO 31000, COSO ERM and APRA's CPS 220 all expect appetite to drive decisions; the test is whether your appetite would change a decision today.

### Q003 — Too Many Controls, Still Not Safe

| Tag | Value |
|---|---|
| Domain | Risk |
| Effort | Project |
| Duration | 3-6 months |
| Tier | Tactical |
| Regulator pressure | Low |
| Cost | Medium ($$) |
| ROI horizon | Mid |
| Leadership traits | Accountability · Technical |

**Plain-language question:** How do you reduce control fatigue without reducing safety?

**Answer:**

More controls do not equal more safety. After ten years of control proliferation, most large organisations carry a portfolio that is duplicative, manual, and weakly tested - which fails twice: it absorbs effort and it misses real exposures.

Start with a controls inventory organised by *risk*, not by team. Rate each control on cost versus risk-reduction. Designate key controls (the ones that, if they failed, would matter) and supporting controls (everything else). Test key controls for design and operational effectiveness twice a year; reduce supporting controls to a lighter cycle or retire them. Map controls to actual incidents from the last three years - any control not implicated in any incident or audit finding is a candidate for retirement, automation, or merging.

Automate low-value manual checks. Limit evidence collection to "show me once" rather than monthly recertification. ISO 27001 Annex A and NIST CSF both expect control rationalisation as the framework matures. The mature signal is fewer, better-tested controls owned by named people, not a 600-line control library no-one reads.

### Q004 — Everyone's Reporting Incidents Differently

| Tag | Value |
|---|---|
| Domain | Risk |
| Effort | Moderate |
| Duration | 6-12 weeks |
| Tier | Foundational |
| Regulator pressure | High |
| Cost | Low ($) |
| ROI horizon | Quick |
| Leadership traits | Accountability · Change · Collaboration |

**Plain-language question:** How do you standardise incident reporting across the business?

**Answer:**

Inconsistent incident reporting hides systemic risk. When operations, IT, HR and front-line all use different forms, severity scales and timelines, the data cannot be aggregated and root causes stay invisible.

The fix is one form, one severity scale, one process - mobile-friendly, accessible via QR code from any workstation. Severity levels need worked examples ("a customer data exposure of any size is Sev 1; a single failed login attempt is Sev 4") so people stop debating definitions. Auto-notify risk, IT and comms on critical incidents within minutes, not days. Capture near-misses, not just losses - APRA CPS 230 and ISO 22301 both expect this.

Provide feedback to the reporter within five business days; nothing kills a reporting culture faster than silence. Hold a monthly incident triage huddle to identify themes. Trend dashboards by category and root cause, not raw count. Include contractors and vendors in scope. Recognise teams for proactive reporting publicly - this is the cheapest culture intervention available and the most underused.

### Q005 — Risk Culture Is Just a Buzzword

| Tag | Value |
|---|---|
| Domain | Risk |
| Effort | Project |
| Duration | 3-6 months |
| Tier | Strategic |
| Regulator pressure | None |
| Cost | Low ($) |
| ROI horizon | Mid |
| Leadership traits | Change · Collaboration · Strategic |

**Plain-language question:** How do you turn risk culture from a buzzword into measurable behaviour?

**Answer:**

"Risk culture" becomes a buzzword when it is treated as a survey result rather than an outcome of leadership behaviour. Culture is what people do when no-one is watching - and that is shaped by what leaders ask about, what gets escalated, and what gets rewarded.

Define three to four observable risk behaviours - "I escalate when uncertain", "I challenge a decision before approval", "I report near-misses". Include risk questions in performance reviews so behaviour is visible in calibration discussions. Start operating meetings with a "risk on the radar" round where each member names one emerging concern; this normalises the conversation.

Celebrate escalation publicly. Share near-miss stories alongside success stories. Train leaders to model transparency, not just sponsor culture decks. Pulse-survey speak-up climate quarterly. APRA's risk culture guidance, the FCA's senior managers regime, and the OCC's heightened standards all expect culture metrics in board reporting. The mature organisation can answer "what changed in our risk behaviour this quarter?" with specifics, not aspirations.

### Q006 — Our Risk Reports Are Too Technical for the Board

| Tag | Value |
|---|---|
| Domain | Risk |
| Effort | Moderate |
| Duration | 2-6 weeks |
| Tier | Foundational |
| Regulator pressure | Low |
| Cost | Low ($) |
| ROI horizon | Quick |
| Leadership traits | Technical · Strategic |

**Plain-language question:** How do you make risk reports the board actually engages with?

**Answer:**

If the board glazes over your risk pack, the problem is not the board - it is the report. Most risk reports are written for the risk function reading the risk function. Boards need decisions, not detail.

Lead with three key messages on one slide. Use plain language and trend arrows; replace heatmaps with risk-reward graphs that show appetite zones. Tie every risk to a strategic objective or value driver so the board can see what it threatens. Include the decision being asked for - "for noting", "for action", "for endorsement" - on every item. Quantify in dollars or operational impact where the data supports it; "high impact" is a qualifier, not an answer.

For top risks, add a brief scenario: what happens if this risk eventuates, what is the response, what is the residual exposure. Pre-brief the chair on red items so the meeting can debate substance, not interpret colours. Close the loop on prior actions. APRA's CPS 220 and the King IV code both expect board risk papers to drive decisions - test yours by asking what decision the last paper triggered.

### Q007 — Who Owns This Risk? Finger-Pointing Everywhere

| Tag | Value |
|---|---|
| Domain | Risk |
| Effort | Moderate |
| Duration | 2-6 weeks |
| Tier | Foundational |
| Regulator pressure | None |
| Cost | Low ($) |
| ROI horizon | Quick |
| Leadership traits | Accountability · Change |

**Plain-language question:** How do you fix unclear risk ownership and finger-pointing?

**Answer:**

When a risk eventuates and four people each say "that was someone else's", the underlying issue is unclear ownership before the fact, not blame after it. Risks without owners are risks no-one is managing.

Map every material risk to a process owner - not the risk team, not a committee. Document the difference between owner (accountable for the outcome) and facilitator (the risk team supporting the assessment). Use RACI for shared risks where two functions both have skin in the game. Include risk ownership in job descriptions and performance objectives so it is part of the role, not a side task.

Escalate any risk that has been unassigned for thirty days; an unowned risk after a month is a governance failure. Review ownership during organisation changes - the most common source of orphaned risks is reorgs that reassign people but not their risks. Recognise owners who measurably reduce exposure. Publish an owner directory. The three-lines-of-defence model, ISO 31000 and CPS 230 all anchor on accountability; the test is whether you can name a single accountable owner for any top-ten risk in under ten seconds.

### Q009 — Controls Pass Design but Fail in Operation

| Tag | Value |
|---|---|
| Domain | Risk |
| Effort | Project |
| Duration | 3-6 months |
| Tier | Tactical |
| Regulator pressure | Low |
| Cost | Medium ($$) |
| ROI horizon | Mid |
| Leadership traits | Technical |

**Plain-language question:** How do you bridge controls that pass design testing but fail in operation?

**Answer:**

A control that is well-designed but operationally weak is more dangerous than a missing control - it gives false comfort. The design-versus-operation gap is the single most common finding in mature audit programmes.

Separate design effectiveness (DE) and operating effectiveness (OE) explicitly in your testing approach; they answer different questions. DE asks "would this work if performed as designed?" OE asks "is it being performed as designed, consistently, by trained people?" Use sample-based testing, not self-attestation. Validate evidence source authenticity - was the screenshot really from the production system on that date.

Include front-line staff in walkthroughs; they often surface workarounds the control owner does not know about. Test during peak and off-peak periods - some controls only fail under stress. Map failure root cause to people, technology, or process. Re-test high-risk failures within 30 days. Feed results into training and SOP updates so the failure mode is closed across the population, not just the sample. SOC 2, ISO 27001, and SOX all expect dual DE/OE evidence; if your control library does not distinguish them, the testing programme is half-built.

### Q010 — Third-Party Risk Is a Black Box

| Tag | Value |
|---|---|
| Domain | Risk |
| Effort | Project |
| Duration | 3-6 months |
| Tier | Strategic |
| Regulator pressure | High |
| Cost | Medium ($$) |
| ROI horizon | Mid |
| Leadership traits | Accountability · Change · Technical |

**Plain-language question:** How do you make third-party risk visible across a vendor portfolio?

**Answer:**

Third-party risk is a black box when every vendor is treated the same. Tier-one critical vendors and tier-three SaaS subscriptions need different rigour - applying uniform process to either is wasteful at one end and dangerous at the other.

Tier vendors by criticality and data access. Require upfront security and insurance evidence before contracting. Use a standard questionnaire (SIG, CAIQ, or your own short form) so responses are comparable. Review contracts for audit rights, SLAs, breach notification windows, and exit clauses. For tier-one vendors, conduct on-site or virtual audits annually; for everyone else, accept SOC 2 or ISO 27001 reports.

Monitor news and sanctions feeds monthly; concentration and geopolitical exposures change faster than annual review cycles. Track vendor-side incident reports - a vendor that hides incidents is a vendor exiting your portfolio. Build exit and contingency plans for tier-one vendors before you need them. APRA's CPS 230, the EU's DORA, and OCC's third-party risk guidance all expect tiered, evidence-based, continuously monitored vendor management - not a once-a-year questionnaire blast.

### Q013 — We Can't Prioritise Projects-Too Many Risk Scores

| Tag | Value |
|---|---|
| Domain | Risk |
| Effort | Project |
| Duration | 6-12 weeks |
| Tier | Tactical |
| Regulator pressure | None |
| Cost | Low ($) |
| ROI horizon | Mid |
| Leadership traits | Strategic |

**Plain-language question:** How do you prioritise projects when every project has a high risk score?

**Answer:**

When every project is rated "high risk", the scoring has stopped discriminating. The fix is not finer scoring - it is a forcing function that produces a ranked, capacity-bounded list.

Use a common 1-5 scale across the portfolio. Apply weighted criteria - cost, time, benefits, risk, strategic fit - so the score reflects more than risk alone. Introduce a "must-win" strategic filter: any project the CEO or strategy team flags as critical bypasses pure scoring but is named explicitly. Map projects on a risk-reward matrix; this surfaces high-reward / acceptable-risk bets that pure risk scoring would suppress.

Cap portfolio work-in-progress to actual delivery capacity. A portfolio of 40 active projects with capacity for 12 will deliver 12 well or 40 badly. Review scores monthly, not at gate-stage. Involve finance for ROI reality-checks. Escalate ties to a steering group with a deadline, not a debate. The PMI standards and the Project Risk Management Institute frameworks both anchor on portfolio capacity, not project-by-project optimism. The test is whether the list of active projects shrinks during prioritisation.

### Q016 — Post-Incident Reviews Blame, Not Learn

| Tag | Value |
|---|---|
| Domain | Risk |
| Effort | Moderate |
| Duration | 2-6 weeks |
| Tier | Foundational |
| Regulator pressure | Low |
| Cost | Low ($) |
| ROI horizon | Quick |
| Leadership traits | Accountability · Collaboration |

**Plain-language question:** How do you run post-incident reviews that produce learning, not blame?

**Answer:**

Post-incident reviews fail when they become disciplinary processes. People stop reporting, stop participating honestly, and the organisation loses the data it most needs to improve.

Adopt a blameless review format - the question is "what allowed this to happen", not "who is at fault". Separate accountability conversations from learning conversations; both are legitimate, but mixing them poisons the learning one. Build a simple template: timeline, what we knew, what we did, what we should have done, what changes prevent recurrence. Include the affected team in the review, not just leadership.

Identify systemic causes versus individual ones - most incidents have multiple contributing systemic factors. Track resulting actions in the same system as audit and risk actions so they do not get lost. Share learning broadly, with names and identifying details removed. Hold a six-month look-back on whether the recurrence prevention actually worked. The aviation industry, healthcare safety frameworks, NIST CSF Recover function, and ISO 22301 all model this; the test is whether incident reporting goes *up* in the year after a major incident review - that signals trust in the process.

### Q018 — Risk Workshop Fatigue

| Tag | Value |
|---|---|
| Domain | Risk |
| Effort | Quick Win |
| Duration | < 2 weeks |
| Tier | Foundational |
| Regulator pressure | None |
| Cost | Low ($) |
| ROI horizon | Quick |
| Leadership traits | Change · Collaboration |

**Plain-language question:** How do you avoid risk workshop fatigue and still get good information?

**Answer:**

If the same eight people sit through quarterly risk workshops repeating the same conversations, the workshops are surfacing nothing new. Fatigue is a signal that the format has decayed.

Replace generic workshops with targeted, time-boxed sessions tied to a specific decision - a project gate, a strategy review, an incident lookback. Keep sessions to 60 minutes with a clear pre-read. Rotate facilitators so the format does not become predictable. Use scenarios and pre-mortems rather than open "what could go wrong" prompts; specificity surfaces better risks.

**Plain-language question:** How do you build KRIs that actually predict, not just describe?

**Answer:**

Most "KRIs" are lagging indicators relabelled - incident counts, audit findings, loss totals. They tell you what has already happened, not what is about to. Predictive KRIs are different by design.

Scenario analysis becomes academic when the scenarios are abstract, the outputs are paragraph-form narratives, and the recipient cannot extract a decision. The fix is in the design, not the modelling.

Anchor scenarios on real plausible events, not extreme tail risks. "What if our largest customer goes into voluntary administration" is more decision-useful than "what if a 1-in-200-year credit event occurs". Build a small number of scenarios deeply rather than many shallowly.

Quantify outputs in the units the decision-maker cares about - dollars of impact, days of disruption, customers affected, regulatory exposure - and compare against appetite thresholds and current capacity. Time-phase the impact: what happens at day 1, week 1, month 1, quarter 1.

Pair every scenario with a decision question: what would we do, what would we wish we had done, what would we want to be true. Test the response plan; if a scenario reveals the response is undefined, that is the finding.

Update scenarios when the world changes; pre-2020 pandemic scenarios understated almost every parameter that mattered. The Bank of England's stress testing approach, the IFRS 9 / CECL provisioning framework, and ISO 22301 all expect scenarios to drive decisions. The test is whether the last scenario analysis changed a budget, a plan, or a treatment - if not, it was academic.

**Plain-language question:** How do you make quantitative risk analysis useful instead of confusing?

### Q054 — Risk Assessments Done, Then Shelved

| Tag | Value |
|---|---|
| Domain | Risk |
| Effort | Moderate |
| Duration | 2-6 weeks |
| Tier | Foundational |
| Regulator pressure | Low |
| Cost | Low ($) |
| ROI horizon | Quick |
| Leadership traits | Accountability · Change |

**Plain-language question:** How do you stop risk assessments from being completed and then ignored?

**Answer:**

A risk assessment that produces no action is process for its own sake. It consumes effort, generates documentation, and changes nothing - which trains the organisation to treat assessments as compliance, not insight.

End every assessment with a treatment plan, not a rating. The rating is an input; the plan is the output. If the plan is "accept" the rationale and the residual exposure must be documented, with a re-assessment trigger.

Connect assessments to the business calendar. Risk assessments performed at the wrong time - after a decision is made, after a project has launched - have no decision to influence. Time them to inform.

Close the loop. Six months after assessment, audit a sample: was the treatment implemented, did it work, did the residual exposure match the assessment. Pattern-recognition surfaces structural problems with the assessment process itself.

ISO 31000, COSO ERM, and APRA CPS 230 all expect treatment to follow assessment. The test is what percentage of last quarter's assessments produced an action that was implemented - if that number is below 70%, the assessment cycle is broken.

**Answer:**

Most root cause analysis stops at the proximate cause - the script that failed, the staff member who skipped the check, the patch that was missed. Real root causes sit one or two layers deeper, in design choices, incentive structures, and organisational decisions.

Use systematic technique. The "five whys" is a starting point; the bow-tie analysis, fault tree analysis, and the Cynefin sense-making framework go further. Pick a method that suits the incident category and apply it consistently.

**Plain-language question:** How do you prevent KPI gaming when targets drive behaviour?

**Answer:**

KPI gaming is the predictable consequence of any well-incentivised single metric. Goodhart's law applies: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. The countermeasure is structural, not motivational.

Pair every primary KPI with a counterweight metric. Volume targets paired with quality measures. Speed targets paired with rework rates. Sales targets paired with customer outcomes. The pair makes one-dimensional gaming visible.

Apply outlier analysis. KPI achievement that is statistically improbable warrants investigation, not just celebration. Performance distributions tighten under gaming; the right tail compresses unnaturally.

Audit selectively. Test the underlying activity behind reported KPI achievement. Auditors looking at whether the KPI is being met as designed, not just achieved on paper, surface gaming patterns.

Listen to the front line. Staff often know which KPIs are being gamed and how; the absence of gaming reports often signals fear of speak-up rather than absence of gaming.

Address the root incentive. If KPI gaming is occurring at scale, the incentive system is mis-designed; reaching for behaviour interventions misses the structural cause.

**Plain-language question:** How do you prevent cryptographic key mismanagement that compromises security?

**Answer:**

Cryptographic keys are the single point of failure for most modern security architecture. Encrypt the data correctly, manage the key badly, and the encryption is decorative.

Maintain a key inventory. Every key in use, its purpose, owner, rotation schedule, and storage location. Ungoverned keys generated by individual teams are the largest source of risk.

Close the loop. After resolution, communicate what happened, what was done, and what is being done to prevent recurrence. This is the last impression and is disproportionately remembered.

The test is whether your last incident response had a customer message ready within an hour of confirmation.

### Q080 — Resilience vs BCM Confusion

| Tag | Value |
|---|---|
| Domain | Resilience |
| Effort | Project |
| Duration | 3-6 months |
| Tier | Strategic |
| Regulator pressure | High |
| Cost | Medium ($$) |
| ROI horizon | Mid |
| Leadership traits | Change |

**Plain-language question:** How do you distinguish operational resilience from business continuity?

**Answer:**

Resilience and BCM overlap but are not the same. Conflating them produces gaps; treating them as identical produces duplication.

Business continuity protects the ability to continue operations during disruption. Its unit is the business process or IT system, and its measure is recovery time and recovery point.

Operational resilience protects the ability to continue delivering important business services to customers and to the financial system. Its unit is the end-to-end customer service, and its measure is impact tolerance - how long can this service be unavailable before harm to customers becomes unacceptable.

Resilience requires mapping important business services across people, process, technology, third parties, and data. BCM scope is narrower. Resilience requires impact tolerances set by the board against customer outcomes; BCM requires RTOs/RPOs set against operational requirements. Resilience requires testing severe but plausible scenarios end-to-end; BCM testing is often process-by-process.

Build resilience as a framework *over* BCM, not instead of it. The BCM artefacts are inputs; the resilience programme integrates them into service-level views.

The Bank of England's SS1/21, the EU's DORA, APRA's CPS 230, and ISO 22316 all anchor here. The test is whether the board can answer "what services matter most to our customers, how long can each be down, and what tested capability supports that."

**Plain-language question:** How do you respond to a social-media reputation shock?

**Answer:**

Social media incidents move at minutes, not days. A coordinated response inside the first two hours often determines whether an issue dies as a flash or becomes a multi-week reputation event.

Maintain monitoring continuously. Brand-mention alerting, sentiment tracking, key-influencer awareness, and crisis-keyword detection should run as standing capability, not be set up reactively.

Pre-define triggers and response thresholds. What level of discussion triggers communications team awareness, what triggers leadership awareness, what triggers public response. The thresholds and the responses are pre-decided.

Coordinate the response across legal, comms, executive, and customer-facing teams. The first response must be true, must avoid creating new problems, and must be aligned with what the customer service team is saying. A divergent message between channels accelerates the issue.

Plan for executive visibility. Whether the CEO speaks is a decision; an unprepared spokesperson making it worse is more common than silence.

### Q088 — Energy-Supply Disruption Risk

| Tag | Value |

| Effort | Project |

| Tier | Strategic |
| Regulator pressure | Low |
| Cost | Medium ($$) |

**Plain-language question:** How do you prepare for energy-supply disruption affecting operations?

**Answer:**

Energy supply disruption was historically a low-frequency risk in stable markets. Recent years have demonstrated it is no longer that. Gas market disruption in Europe, grid stress in California, weather events in Texas and Australia, and geopolitical pressure on multiple energy sources have all caused operational disruption to organisations that thought they were covered.

Map energy dependencies. Direct (your facilities), indirect (data centres, vendor operations, logistics) and concentration (multiple critical operations on the same regional grid) all create exposure.

Test backup. Generators, UPS systems, and battery backups need regular load testing, not paper certification. Many organisations discover during real outages that backup systems sized years ago no longer support current load.

Plan for extended disruption, not just brief outages. Most BCP scenarios assume hours; extended energy disruption may run days. Fuel logistics for generators, staff welfare, and prioritised load-shedding all matter.

Coordinate with critical vendors. Cloud providers, payment processors, and telcos have their own resilience but operate in regional grids; understand their plans for your operating regions.

Address energy cost volatility separately from supply. Cost shocks affect margin; supply shocks affect operations. The risks are related but require different treatments.

The IEA energy security frameworks, ISO 22301, the EU's DORA (for financial services), and major industry-specific resilience guidance all anchor here. The test is whether you have rehearsed a 72-hour regional energy disruption scenario in the last 18 months.

**Plain-language question:** How do you assess environmental hazard exposure across your operations?

**Answer:**

Environmental hazard exposure - flood, fire, storm, heatwave, drought, sea level rise, infrastructure failure caused by climate stress - is moving from low-frequency to higher-frequency risk in many operating regions.

Map facilities and operations against hazard zones. Flood maps, bushfire maps, cyclone tracks, seismic zones, heat-stress projections. Multiple datasets - government, scientific, insurance - provide overlapping views.

Address direct and indirect exposure. Your facility may not be in the hazard zone but your suppliers, transport corridors, data centres, and workforce may be. Indirect exposure often exceeds direct.

Apply forward-looking analysis, not just historical. Climate-driven hazard frequency is rising; historical data understates future risk. The TCFD and ISSB frameworks provide methodology for forward-looking scenario analysis.

Address employee welfare. Heatwaves, smoke, flood evacuation, and infrastructure stress affect staff capability. Plans that protect operations but ignore employee welfare fail.

Coordinate with insurers. Hazard zones affect insurability; insurance markets are tightening for high-exposure regions.

The IPCC reports, TCFD, ISSB IFRS S2, ISO 14091, and major reinsurer hazard databases all anchor here. The test is whether your top three operational hazards are mapped, scenario-tested, and have current response plans.

### Q096 — Black-Swan Planning Absent

| Tag | Value |
|---|---|
| Domain | Resilience |
| Effort | Transformation |
| Duration | > 6 months |
| Tier | Transformational |
| Regulator pressure | Low |
| Cost | Medium ($$) |
| ROI horizon | Strategic |
| Leadership traits | Strategic |

**Plain-language question:** How do you plan for black-swan and high-impact low-probability events?

**Answer:**

Black-swan planning is genuinely hard because the events are by definition outside historical patterns. The temptation is to ignore them; the cost of ignoring them is borne when one materialises.

Build optionality, not specific predictions. The goal is not to predict the next pandemic, geopolitical shock, or systemic technology failure - it is to ensure operational resilience to a class of severe disruption. Diversified suppliers, distributed operations, financial buffers, and rehearsed crisis processes all reduce vulnerability without requiring prediction.

Run pre-mortems on plausible severe scenarios. Imagine the organisation has been disrupted for 90 days; work backward to what should have been in place. The exercise surfaces gaps that bottom-up risk assessment misses.

Maintain financial buffers. Liquidity, alternative funding sources, and contingency reserves provide time to respond when the operating environment changes faster than business model can adapt.

Stress test against compound scenarios, not single events. A pandemic plus a cyber attack plus regulatory action is more realistic than any single scenario.

Avoid normalising historical patterns. The 100-year flood is now arriving more often; the 1-in-200 cyber event is reachable in tighter cycles. Calibration must be forward-looking.

The Bank of England's stress testing approach, ISO 22301, and the WEF Global Risks Report all anchor here. The test is whether the organisation has explicitly considered three severe-but-plausible scenarios in the last 12 months.

**Plain-language question:** How do you start governing AI use that is already happening across the business?

**Answer:**

AI is being used inside most organisations whether or not there is a governance programme. Waiting for a perfect framework before acting means governing nothing while exposure grows.

### Q078 — Model-Risk Unmanaged

| Tag | Value |
|---|---|
| Domain | AI |
| Effort | Project |
| Duration | 3-6 months |
| Tier | Strategic |
| Regulator pressure | High |
| Cost | Medium ($$) |
| ROI horizon | Mid |
| Leadership traits | Technical · Strategic |

**Plain-language question:** How do you manage model risk in analytical and AI-driven systems?

**Answer:**

Model risk - the risk of loss or wrong decisions caused by errors in models - is mature in banking and immature elsewhere. As organisations adopt AI and analytical models more widely, model risk becomes everyone's problem, and the banking discipline is a useful template.

Maintain a model inventory. Every model with material decision impact needs a record - purpose, input data, methodology, validation, owner, last review.

Apply tiered governance based on materiality. High-impact models (credit decisions, pricing, capital allocation, regulatory calculation) require independent validation, documented testing, ongoing monitoring, and formal sign-off. Lower-impact models need lighter governance but still need oversight.

Validate independently. The model developer cannot be the validator; the conflict of interest is structural. Validation tests assumptions, data, methodology, implementation, and stability over time.

Monitor for drift. Models built on historical data degrade as the world changes; ongoing monitoring against actual outcomes detects drift before it produces material errors.

Address AI-specific issues - bias, explainability, robustness to adversarial input, and behaviour on out-of-distribution data. These are model-risk issues even when the term is unfamiliar.

The Federal Reserve's SR 11-7 (model risk management), the OCC bulletins on model risk, ISO/IEC 42001, the NIST AI RMF, and the EU AI Act all anchor on this discipline. The test is whether your highest-impact AI or analytical model has documented validation, monitoring, and a defined owner.

### Q085 — Ethical-AI Concerns Unchecked

| Tag | Value |

**Plain-language question:** How do you address ethical AI concerns without slowing AI delivery?

**Answer:**

Ethical AI concerns are real, growing, and increasingly regulated. Treating them as a separate compliance gate slows delivery; embedding them into the AI development lifecycle keeps speed and addresses risk.

Define principles up front - fairness, transparency, accountability, privacy, robustness, human oversight. Make them specific enough to drive design choices, not just decorative statements.

Apply tiered governance based on impact. High-impact AI systems (consequential decisions about people, safety-critical applications, regulated activities) need impact assessments, bias testing, explainability requirements, and human-in-the-loop design. Lower-impact systems need lighter touch but are not exempt.

Build assessment into the development process, not as a gate at the end. Privacy impact assessments, fairness testing, robustness testing, and security review happen as the system is built. Late-stage findings are expensive to remediate.

Address training data. Provenance, licensing, representativeness, and consent all matter; many ethical AI failures trace back to training data choices.

Maintain meaningful human oversight scaled to impact. Engage stakeholders affected by AI decisions; transparency and avenues for challenge build trust and surface issues early.

### Q094 — Generative-AI Misuse Risk

| Tag | Value |
|---|---|
| Domain | AI |
| Effort | Moderate |
| Duration | 6-12 weeks |
| Tier | Tactical |
| Regulator pressure | Moderate |
| Cost | Low ($) |
| ROI horizon | Quick |
| Leadership traits | Change · Technical |

**Plain-language question:** How do you control generative AI misuse risk while enabling productive use?

**Answer:**

Generative AI is in use across the organisation regardless of policy. The question is whether the use is governed or shadow; the answer is governance, not prohibition.

Provide approved tools. Enterprise-grade generative AI with proper data handling, access controls, and audit logging meets most legitimate business needs. People reach for shadow AI when the official channel is absent or too restrictive.

Define clear use rules. What data may be input (no customer data, no confidential, no regulated). What outputs require human review (anything customer-facing, anything safety-related, anything regulated). What outputs may not be used at all.

Train staff. Generative AI is a new tool; people misuse it because they do not understand its limitations. Hallucinations, prompt injection, training data leakage, and over-reliance on output are all addressable through training.

Apply technical controls. DLP rules to prevent confidential data being pasted into public AI tools, browser extensions to monitor or block public AI use on managed devices, prompt logging for compliance review.

Govern AI development distinctly - data governance, model risk management, ethical review, monitoring.

The EU AI Act, US executive orders, ISO/IEC 42001, and the NIST AI RMF all set substantive expectations. The test is whether your AI policy was last updated within six months - if not, it is behind the threat.
